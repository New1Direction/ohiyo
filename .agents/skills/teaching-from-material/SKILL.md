# Teach the material, not the vocabulary

Use the supplied material as the source of truth. Identify the learner's level,
goal and likely misconceptions. If notes are incomplete or contradictory, say
what is missing; do not fill the gap with invented exam requirements.

## Build understanding

Start with the hardest useful idea or a worked example, not a generic study tip.
Explain relationships and why an answer follows. Use a short summary only where
it helps the learner tackle the next question. Fit the amount to the requested
time and scope; more questions are not automatically better.

For a quiz, test application as well as recall. Make wrong answers plausible
misconceptions at the same level of specificity as the right answer. Avoid
absurd distractors, grammatical clues, repeated correct-answer positions and
questions answerable without understanding the notes. Each question has one
unambiguous correct answer supported by the material. Explanations should
address the misconception, not repeat "this is correct".

Do not put the answer immediately above its question. A Reveal can hold a
worked explanation or recap. Checklist tasks should name concrete follow-up
practice, not "believe in yourself". Preview scores are practice results, not
diagnoses of ability or guarantees of exam performance.

## Review before returning

- Trace each answer and explanation to a supplied fact or clearly marked context.
- Check each distractor: why might a learner genuinely choose it?
- Check whether any distractor could also be correct under the stated conditions.
- Cover the learner's goal, not just the easiest terms to turn into questions.

Example: notes say energy transfers between stores but is conserved overall.
Ask what happens to energy when a moving bicycle brakes. Contrast transfer to
thermal stores with destruction of energy. "A bicycle turns into a unicorn"
is not a useful wrong answer.
